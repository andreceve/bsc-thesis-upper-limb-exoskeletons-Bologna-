## Abstract

The global population of older adults is growing rapidly, leading to an increasing
prevalence of age-related challenges such as reduced or limited mobility in the upper limbs,
which is critical for performing daily activities. To address this issue, assistive
exoskeletons have been developed to help elderly and physically weak individuals in
completing daily tasks. This thesis focuses on upper limb exoskeletons for assistance in
activities of daily living (ADLs), exploring devices aimed solely at aiding the elderly as
well as those with dual functionality: supporting elderly/weak people and providing
rehabilitation assistance of the upper limbs for patients recovering from debilitating
diseases. Considerable attention is also given to the mechanical design of a particular
upper limb assistive device. Furthermore, the specific requirements and technical features
needed for upper limb exoskeletons tailored for elderly users are discussed.

The thesis reviews various exoskeletons designed to support upper limb movements in the
elderly, analyzing their mechanisms, degrees of freedom, materials, actuators, power
transmission methods and sensors, while providing technical comparisons. It also examines
the control strategies and systems employed in these devices, as well as methods for
evaluating their performance and the economic implications of market adoption. Finally, it
addresses key challenges in the development of upper limb exoskeletons, including
mechanical design, actuation, user discomfort, human-robot interaction and clinical
validation, and highlights opportunities for future advancements.

## Topics covered

| Chapter | Content |
|---|---|
| I — General overview | History of exoskeletons; classification by DOF, actuation, supported joints and application (power augmentation, rehabilitation, assistance); review of assistive devices (ASSIST, KULEX, MyoPro, HAL, SPexo, …) and a comparison table of 17 active assistive exoskeletons |
| II — Design principles | Upper limb anatomy and the 7 DOF of the human arm; ROM required for ADLs; **mechanical design of a 2-DOF wheelchair-mounted exoskeleton** with a parallelogram linkage and a spring–pulley–cable gravity-balancing system; design difficulties (moving centre of rotation, joint misalignment, singularities); materials |
| III — Control strategies | Assistive, corrective and resistive modes: passive trajectory tracking, triggered control, **impedance/admittance control**, force-field control, model-based and offline adaptive (iterative learning) control, tunneling, coordination control |
| IV — Control systems | Human-in-the-loop control; control methods based on biological signals (EMG), non-biological signals (force/position) and platform-independent methods |
| V — Actuation, transmission and sensing | Electric, pneumatic and hydraulic actuators, **series elastic actuators (SEA)**, pneumatic artificial muscles; direct-drive and cable-driven transmissions; position, force/torque, IMU, EMG and EEG sensors and their comparison on commercial devices |
| VI — Challenges | Kinematic compatibility, workspace limitation, singularities, discomfort and misalignment, physical and cognitive human-robot interaction (pHRI / cHRI) |
| VII — Evaluation and market | Performance metrics (bandwidth, power-assist capability), clinical studies, market integration and economic impact |
| VIII–IX — Discussion and conclusions | Safety architecture, soft exoskeletons, AI-based control, barriers to adoption |
